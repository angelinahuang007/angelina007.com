Rendering Equation Deep Dive

Understanding the fundamental equation of computer graphics and how different BRDF models approximate light reflection.

The Rendering Equation

Lo(x, ωo) = Le(x, ωo) + ∫Ω fr(x, ωi, ωo) Li(x, ωi) (ωi · n) dωi

Term Definitions

Lo(x, ωo)

Outgoing radiance from point x in direction ωo. This is what the camera sees.

Le(x, ωo)

Emitted radiance directly from the surface itself (e.g., self-luminous materials).

fr(x, ωi, ωo)

BRDF (Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function). Describes how the surface reflects light from ωi to ωo.

Li(x, ωi)

Incoming radiance from direction ωi. Light arriving at the surface.

ωi · n

Cosine of the angle between incoming light direction and surface normal. Accounts for foreshortening.

Ω (hemisphere)

Integration domain: all directions above the surface (2π steradians).

Classic BRDF Models

Lambert

Ideal diffuse reflection. Light scatters uniformly in all directions equally.

Blinn-Phong

Specular + diffuse. Creates glossy highlights, computationally efficient.

Cook-Torrance

Microfacet-based PBR. Physically plausible for metals and rough dielectrics.

Oren-Nayar

Rough diffuse surfaces. More realistic than Lambert for non-smooth materials.

Lambert (Diffuse Reflection)

fr = c / π

The simplest BRDF model. Light is scattered equally in all directions above the surface. The 1/π normalization ensures energy conservation. Commonly used for matte surfaces like paper or fabric.

0.80

Parameters

  • c (albedo): Surface color/reflectance (0–1 for each channel)
  • No roughness: Diffuse always looks the same regardless of viewing angle
  • Best for: Matte surfaces, unpolished wood, paper, fabric
Note: Lambert looks flat under all lighting conditions. It doesn't capture the subtle directional variation that Oren-Nayar provides for rough surfaces.

Blinn-Phong (Specular + Diffuse)

fr = cd / π + cs (n + 2) / (2π) (h · n)n

Combines diffuse and specular components. The specular term creates a glossy highlight that moves with the viewing angle. The exponent n controls the size of the highlight—higher values = sharper, smaller highlights (more polished).

0.50
0.80
0.50

Parameters

  • cd (diffuse): Matte base color
  • cs (specular): Highlight color/intensity
  • n (exponent): Shininess. High n = sharp highlight, n=1 = very rough
  • h: Half-vector between light and view direction
  • Best for: Plastic, lacquered surfaces, quick approximations
Note: Fast to compute and intuitive to control, but not physically based. Does not conserve energy and breaks down at grazing angles.

Cook-Torrance (Microfacet PBR)

fr = cd / π + cs DFG / (4(ωo·n)(ωi·n))

Physically based model treating surfaces as collections of tiny microfacets. D = roughness distribution, F = Fresnel reflection, G = geometric attenuation. Industry standard for realistic metal and material rendering.

0.50
0.80
0.30

Parameters

  • roughness (α): Surface roughness (0=mirror smooth, 1=very rough)
  • metallic: 0 = dielectric, 1 = conductor (controls base reflectance)
  • D (GGX): Trowbridge-Reitz distribution for microfacet slopes
  • F (Schlick): Fresnel effect—reflectance increases at grazing angles
  • G (SmithGGX): Self-shadowing of microfacets
  • Best for: All surfaces, metals, ceramics, realistic materials
Note: Physically plausible and energy-conserving. Used in game engines (PBR workflow) and offline renderers. More complex but produces realistic results.

Oren-Nayar (Rough Diffuse)

fr = c / π (A + B cos(φi - φo) sin(α) tan(β))

Extends Lambert for rough diffuse surfaces. Surfaces appear brighter when viewing angle aligns with light direction. Better matches real-world materials like concrete, sand, and unpolished wood.

Parameters

  • σ (roughness): RMS slope of microfacets (0=smooth diffuse, 1=very rough)
  • A, B: Coefficients derived from roughness for the Oren-Nayar formula
  • φ, α, β: Azimuthal angles and elevation angles in hemispheric coordinates
  • Best for: Rough diffuse surfaces, concrete, sand, rough plaster, fabric
Note: More accurate than Lambert for rough surfaces but less complex than Cook-Torrance. Good compromise between realism and computational cost.
